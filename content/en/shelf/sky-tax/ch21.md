# Chapter 21 · The Silent Ground Station

The car was borrowed from a used car dealership in Huaqiangbei — not borrowed, Farhan rented it using a fake identity. A silver-gray Wuling Hongguang, the rear seats removed and replaced with a transmitter loaded in an aviation-grade aluminum case. The car drove on Shenzhen's Bei'huan Boulevard at three in the morning at a speed that was precisely not exceeding the limit, blending in among the night-shift taxis and early-morning freight trucks, utterly unremarkable.

Farhan drove. Lu Yuan sat in the passenger seat, holding a tablet with no network connection, the screen showing a static map. Their destination was an abandoned ground station sixty kilometers north of Shenzhen. On the satellite map, the area was marked as "unused communication facility." Zooming in revealed a white parabolic antenna, about seven meters in diameter, half-hidden in wild grass and shrubs.

"Starlink's asset management system can't find this station anymore," Farhan said, eyes fixed on the road ahead, occasionally glancing at the rearview mirror. "After the third-generation satellites came online, the older Starlink satellites don't need this kind of traditional ground station anymore — they exchange data directly through inter-satellite laser links. Ground stations are just backup access points. But this one uses S-band. It's not compatible with the new satellites. It was marked as 'pending disposal asset' two years ago and went into a cold-storage archive nobody checks."

"But the lines are still there," Lu Yuan said.

"The lines are still there." Farhan repeated. "The physical link was never disconnected. It's too old — old enough to have been forgotten by everyone. Starlink's automated operations system scans the global ground segment asset inventory once a year, but this station's firmware version is too old — it returns a format the new system doesn't recognize during the scan protocol. The scanner classifies it as 'noise' and skips it. Like a person who gets missed in a census because their name is too old, not in this generation's name database. They don't exist legally — but they're still physically alive."

Lu Yuan said nothing. He watched out the window as the landscape transitioned gradually from city lights to dark suburban terrain. Shenzhen's northern boundary was a blurry line in the night — tall buildings growing shorter, sparser, replaced by low industrial factories and vacant land. The gaps between streetlights grew wider and wider, until they disappeared entirely and only the headlights cut a cone-shaped zone of light across the road ahead.

From Shenzhen to this abandoned ground station, the straight-line distance was less than sixty kilometers. But the temporal distance spanned two full generations of communication technology. There was a sense of temporal dislocation — the ground station was built sometime in the 2030s, back when Starlink was still just a few thousand satellites in low Earth orbit and the ground segment still needed large numbers of traditional parabolic antennas to send and receive signals. Now those antennas rusted in the night breeze, their bases overgrown with vines, the roof of the satellite signal processing room collapsed in several places, exposing the steel framework inside.

Farhan parked the car in a small clearing next to the antenna base. When the engine died, the silence was suddenly so complete it was uncomfortable — no urban background noise, no traffic hum, only the rustle of wind through wild grass and the occasional distant chirp of insects.

They got out. The night air was much cooler than in the city, carrying the smell of earth and decaying vegetation. Lu Yuan stood by the car and looked up at the sky — no clouds. The light pollution from Shenzhen's northern suburbs was weaker here; he could see more stars. The real stars should have been brighter than the Starlink satellites, but he found his gaze involuntarily drawn to those steadily moving points of light — they traversed the night sky at regular intervals, like a nearly invisible conveyor belt spanning the entire firmament.

Twelve thousand of them. They looked clearer here than in the city, more dense. Like a carefully woven silver net covering the surface of the sky.

He lowered his head and stopped looking at them.

Farhan took the aviation-grade aluminum case from the trunk. It was heavy — they had to carry it together, one at each end, to move it steadily. They carried it to a semi-buried equipment cabinet next to the antenna base. The cabinet's door lock was rusted solid. Farhan sprayed it twice with penetrating oil, waited a few minutes, then wedged a large screwdriver into the lock gap and pried — the latch snapped open with a metallic crack that quickly dissipated in the night breeze.

The interior was in better shape than he'd expected. The sealing rubber gasket had aged and hardened but hadn't completely failed. The main power lines and signal cables inside were intact, the labels still legible — S-BAND TRANSMIT CHAIN, POLARIZATION CONTROL, POWER AMPLIFIER BIAS SUPPLY. Farhan strapped a headlamp to his forehead and began checking circuit continuity one by one.

Lu Yuan set the aluminum case on the ground and opened the latches. The transmitter inside was a full size larger than the prototype they'd used in the safe house — the casing had been hand-soldered by Amina at a partner workshop in Lagos two weeks ago, then smuggled into the country through the container-ship cargo hold of a freighter docked at Shenzhen port, pre-deployed to the vicinity via Pigeonnet's physical logistics channel. Its design was straightforward: a high-precision arbitrary waveform generator cascaded into a high-power solid-state amplifier, the output connected to a directional antenna covering S-band. Simple, brute-force, with a single purpose — to send a precisely modulated signal to any old-model Starlink satellite that happened to pass overhead.

That signal would contain three things: the QKD noise signature template for activating Milan's backdoor, the first route advertisement packet of the SkyWalker protocol, and a globally synchronized timestamp.

Lu Yuan crouched to inspect the transmitter's power module. He measured the main power rail with a multimeter — stable at 48 volts, ripple within acceptable range. The green indicator light was on. The machine was in standby.

"Are the pigeons in position?" he asked.

Farhan didn't answer immediately. After connecting the cables in his hands, he took an offline tablet from his pocket and opened a static file — the last synchronized update from Amina. The screen showed a world map marked with thirty-two glowing points.

"Thirty-two cities. Thirty-two pigeons. All confirmed at position." Farhan's voice was quieter than usual in the open wilderness, like someone confirming something extremely important who had unconsciously lowered their voice, afraid of startling something. "From Reykjavik to Cape Town, from Santiago to Tokyo — every pigeon arrived at the designated location before dawn local time today. They're all waiting for a signal. Not through phones, not through any electromagnetic method — they have physical storage cards containing SkyWalker's injection firmware. When they receive the activation instruction, they insert the card into an offline terminal and press Enter."

"How is the activation instruction sent?"

"It isn't." Farhan looked up, the headlamp beam sweeping across the thick grass around the ground station. "No signal is sent. The activation time is pre-agreed, hardcoded into each card's injection program. All pigeons across the globe — in the same second, perform the same operation. There's no electromagnetic signal to trace, because the instruction itself doesn't need to be transmitted — it's already written into the storage card. We just need to be in the right place at the right time."

Lu Yuan was silent for a few seconds. He looked at the transmitter's green standby indicator, at the shadow cast by the headlamp beam on the edge of the aluminum case. The elegance of this plan lay in its clumsiness — using the slowest, most primitive method to trigger the fastest, most high-tech process. Transporting storage cards physically. Replacing network synchronization protocols with the precision of human synchronization.

He took out his phone. The screen lit up — zero signal bars. Expected. There were no base stations within kilometers of this abandoned ground station. He opened the cached medical status page in offline mode — the last diagnostic summary Dr. Zhao had screenshot for him before he left.

The screen showed his daughter's latest status. Dr. Zhao had added a note in gray text:

**「The remote consultation has been rescheduled through the emergency channel. Estimated window: approximately 2 hours. It is recommended to keep the account in normal status before this window. If the account is frozen again, the system will automatically enter a 48-hour cooling period.」**

Lu Yuan checked the timestamp at the bottom of the page. That was already hours ago.

He locked the screen and put the phone back in his pocket. Then he crouched down, opened the maintenance panel on the side of the transmitter, and began the final system integration debugging with Farhan.

In Berlin, it was 9:17 PM.

Mia sat on a folding chair in a basement apartment in Kreuzberg. It wasn't a Data Party safe house — it was a private bolt-hole of hers, a single room on the ground floor of an old building with a window facing a courtyard that never received direct sunlight. The room was piled with tech magazines, circuit board remnants, and empty coffee cups. A film enlarger covered in dust stood in the corner.

On the table in front of her was a device. Not a SkyWalker test terminal, not any modified SDR — it was an ancient personal computer, its case an off-white color, the CRT monitor sitting on the desk like a heavy stone. This machine was manufactured around 2035 — before Starlink's total monopoly, in an era when not everything was metered and billed. Its hard drive contained an old FTP server software that Starlink's protocol stack didn't recognize.

This machine was connected to a line. Not fiber optic, not satellite link — an ordinary old telephone line, running down the walls of the old building into Berlin's underground copper telephone network, which still existed. At the other end of that telephone line sat an equally ancient server in an unremarkable office building basement in San Francisco.

It was a private channel Milan Vojnović had configured more than twenty years ago. He had never told anyone about it — not Starlink's official infrastructure, not the Data Party's known secure channels, not any intelligence agency's monitored targets. It was just a copper wire forgotten in the ruins of the telecommunications network, unmaintained but still there.

When Mia was six years old, she had used this computer and this line to draw pictures for her father when he was on a business trip in San Francisco. She used the most primitive drawing software, the mouse tracing crooked lines — a family of three, a sun, a cloud. Then she uploaded them via FTP to a private folder her father had created for her on that server. Milan would open that folder from his business hotel, download the pictures, and reply with a brief email: "Received. Beautiful drawing. Miss you."

The line had never been used again. But Milan had never shut down the FTP server on his end. Mia knew this because once a year, she secretly tested it — using an offline machine, dialing up through that old telephone line, sending a PING request to that IP address everyone had long forgotten. Every time, it returned a response. Every time, the response time increased — the server's hard drive was aging, but it answered.

Tonight, she sat in front of that computer for a long time. The screen's phosphor glow illuminated her face, making her expression look younger than her actual age — like someone who had returned to their childhood room and found everything still in its original place, only they themselves were no longer who they used to be.

She took a black USB storage device from her pocket. The storage card was about the size of a postage stamp, containing only one file — not a letter, a data packet. SkyWalker's global activation code.

She plugged it into the computer's USB port. The machine took several seconds to recognize this modern device — its operating system was too old, needing a compatibility driver to read it. When the file appeared on screen, she stared at it for a moment. The filename was an auto-generated hash, devoid of any emotional significance. But its content — once this string of data was injected into Starlink's core network — would change everything.

She began to operate. First, dial-up. The telephone line's modem emitted an ancient, almost forgotten screech — the sound of the handshake protocol, a noise from the last era of the internet, like the call of some ancient creature transmitted from the depths of the sea.

Then she opened the FTP client.

She typed in that IP address — she hadn't entered it since she was sixteen, but her fingers remembered. Username: her childhood initials. Password: her birthday.

Connection successful.

The screen showed an empty directory. Inside it, a single folder, its name still the same as when she was six — **「Mia's Drawings」**.

She dragged the activation code file into that folder. The upload progress bar crawled slowly — the telephone line's bandwidth was extremely limited; a file of a few hundred KB would take nearly two minutes to transfer. She watched the progress bar move one bar at a time, her fingers resting on the mouse, unmoving.

Upload complete.

The screen displayed: **「File transfer successful · 1 file uploaded to /Mia's Drawings/skywalker_activate.dat」**

Mia closed the FTP client. She didn't disconnect the telephone line — she left the old modem online, like someone standing guard at a door, listening for footsteps on the other side.

She leaned back in her chair. The ceiling of the basement apartment was low — she could almost touch it with an outstretched arm. Berlin's night pressed down on the old building. Through the courtyard's glass, she could see a small patch of deep blue sky — no Starlink satellite tracks passed through that narrow opening above the courtyard, or maybe they did but she didn't look.

She picked up her phone from the table. No signal. But she opened the notes app in offline mode and typed a line:

**「I've sent it. The rest is up to you. Dad.」**

She didn't send this line — there was no network here to send anything. She just wrote it down, as a record. Then she locked the screen and turned the phone face-down on the table.

She sat in front of that old computer, listening to the steady white noise of the modem through the telephone line. She remembered her childhood — when her father came back from business trips, he always brought her a small gift. Sometimes an exotic stone, sometimes a picture book, sometimes just a postcard bought at the airport convenience store. He never said "I brought you a gift" — he just put it on her desk, like someone who didn't want his actions to seem too deliberate.

She had left Belgrade at twenty-two, and she hadn't taken any of those gifts with her. But now she was sitting here, in a windowless basement apartment in Berlin, using the same computer, the same telephone line, the same FTP account, to upload a file to her father's server.

It wasn't a drawing. It was a key.

She didn't know if her father would use that key to open that door.

But she had already put it where he could see it.

In San Francisco, it was 1:11 PM.

Milan was in a meeting. The Starlink North America Operations Center's weekly executive meeting. The agenda had three items: the anomaly audit report on South America's QKD link, a new round of pricing strategy adjustments for the European market, and a "High-Risk User Cluster Control Plan" from the Asia-Pacific Security Center — submitted by Silas, requesting authorization for the security department to have "preventive freezing authority" over Starlink accounts in critical industries such as healthcare, finance, and education.

Milan sat in the middle of the long conference table, a closed laptop and an untouched glass of water in front of him. His gaze was fixed on the presentation being projected by the meeting host, but his fingers, below the table, were operating a phone placed in the inner pocket of his suit jacket using tactile memory alone — screen facing inward, brightness turned to minimum.

He felt the phone vibrate in his pocket. Not a call, not a text, not any message transmitted through the Starlink network. He had set up a dedicated hardware monitor for that telephone line — its only function was to detect whether the FTP server in that San Francisco underground equipment room had received a new upload.

The display showed a notification: **「New file uploaded · Source IP: Berlin dial-up connection · Filename: skywalker_activate.dat」**

Milan sat at the conference table, surrounded by thirteen Starlink executives and department heads. Someone was speaking, about pricing elasticity in the European market. Someone was flipping through printed materials, the paper making a soft rustling sound. Someone was taking meeting notes on a terminal, the keyboard clicks sparse and rhythmic.

He stood up.

"Milan?" The meeting host paused. "Do you have something to add?"

"Stepping out for a moment," he said. His voice was even, without any anomaly. "Personal matter."

He didn't wait for a response. He picked up his closed laptop and walked out of the conference room. The corridor was long, lined with soundproof glass walls. Outside was California sunshine — bright enough to be almost piercing, casting large diamond-shaped patches of light on the white corridor floor. He walked to his office at the end of the corridor, pushed the door open, and locked it.

The office was empty. The blinds were half-closed, sunlight slanting through the gaps, cutting parallel bands of light across the desk.

He didn't sit in his office chair. He stood in front of the desk, took the phone from his inner pocket. The notification was still there. He tapped it.

The system displayed: **「Filename: skywalker_activate.dat · Size: 384 bytes · Uploader: authenticated user from 198.51.100.0/24 (username: mjw_drawings)」**

The username was what Mia had registered on this FTP server when she was little — "mjw_drawings," Milan Vojnović + drawings. A name she'd picked at age six, unchanged for twenty years.

Milan stood in the office, the afternoon sunlight streaming through the blinds, falling across the side of his face, casting a pattern of alternating light and shadow on the opposite wall. He stared at that filename for a long time — so long that the phone screen began to dim from inactivity. He didn't let it go dark. He touched the screen and lit it up again.

384 bytes.

The uninvited guest on the third floor. The chief architect of one hundred and twenty thousand satellites. A child who grew up in the war-torn streets of Belgrade, who became the chief architect of the most powerful communication network in the world. A father whose daughter hadn't contacted him in three years.

He stood inside this shell of labels, holding a phone that displayed a 384-byte filename.

His finger hovered above the screen for a long time — the kind of hovering that carried the same weight as Lu Yuan's finger hovering above the Enter key in that underground safe house in Shenzhen.

He pressed the link to the filename. Not to download — he didn't need to download it to know its content. Just to confirm it was there, accessible, complete, uncorrupted.

Then he turned to his desk. On the desktop, his personal terminal was displaying an icon he looked at every day but had never operated — the status indicator of his nested entry point. A logical address space hidden deep within the system he had designed himself, unrecorded in any routing table.

Its status was: **Idle · Not Activated**

Milan sat down at the terminal. He didn't touch the nested entry point. He opened a plain text editor — not because the terminal had any special tool he needed, but because he needed a blank space with no pre-set framework to write in.

He typed a line. Not through any encrypted channel — he opened an interface to the encrypted text channel leading to Lu Yuan. That channel was one he had quietly established after the Sahel operation — embedded in the backup line Farhan used to contact the Data Party, he had inserted his own listening post. He could read messages sent to Farhan. He had never replied to any of them. He didn't know if he would receive a reply tonight.

He typed. The cursor blinked in the empty input field. Each character he typed was encrypted and transmitted through a side channel hidden in Starlink's core network audit log stream. The transmission rate was extremely low — about one character every three seconds — but speed wasn't the goal. The goal was for this message to be received.

**「I can let you in.」**

He stopped. The cursor blinked at the end of the sentence, like an ear waiting for the other shoe to drop.

**「But I'm coming in too.」**

At that moment, sixty kilometers north of Shenzhen, at the abandoned ground station, the wild grass rustled in the night breeze.

Lu Yuan's phone lit up — not with a regular notification, but with a chime he had never heard before. Not through any conventional channel — it was a test signaling channel of the SkyWalker protocol, which Farhan had set up saying "just in case there's an extreme situation requiring one-way communication." They had never received anything on this channel. Not once.

Now it was ringing.

Lu Yuan put down his multimeter and picked up the phone. The message on the screen was only a few lines — short, no punctuation errors, but the sender's identity was fully confirmed by the channel itself.

Milan Vojnović.

Lu Yuan read the message. Then he handed the phone to Farhan.

Farhan took it and read it. In that moment, his expression was very complex — not complete surprise, because deep down he had anticipated that Milan might make some kind of unexpected choice. But he hadn't anticipated the specific shape of that choice.

Milan's condition was not "don't hurt my daughter." Not "I can help you, but you have to guarantee my safety." Not any calculation driven by self-preservation or fear.

Milan's condition was that he wanted to come in too.

He wanted an observer's seat — not because he wanted to control Data Haven, but because he needed to know whether he could shut it down if necessary.

Farhan looked up at Lu Yuan. In the darkness of the abandoned ground station, neither of them spoke. From somewhere in the distant wild grass came the cry of an unidentified bird — short and clear, like a beat in a moment of silence.

"He might sell us all out," Farhan said.

Lu Yuan took the phone back and reread Milan's message. When he looked at those words, he wasn't thinking about Milan's calculations, Milan's power, Milan's ability to manipulate the Starlink system.

He was thinking about what Mia had said on that rainy night in Lagos — "He's not a bad person. He's just too afraid."

Lu Yuan put the phone back in his pocket.

"He's already sold us out once," he said. His voice was light in the night breeze, but steady. "Those twenty-three people in the Sahel — he used our lives as a test. But that was a one-time thing. He won't sell us out again. Because his daughter is watching."

Farhan didn't argue.

Lu Yuan took another terminal from his pocket — not a phone, a dedicated text communicator for the SkyWalker protocol, with a screen that could only display a few lines of text. He pressed the power button. The screen lit up. The cursor of the input method blinked in the empty input field. He thought of the line Milan had sent — "I'm coming in too." It wasn't a demand. It was a request — wrapped in a shell of power and conditions, a request from one father to another.

He typed his reply. Just a few words. Then he pressed Send.

The message traveled through the noise voids of the QKD channel, passing through a Starlink satellite passing over southern China at that moment, along the routing path defined by Milan's own nested entry point, reaching the receiving end. The transmission rate was about one bit per second. Those few words of the reply took nearly a full minute to arrive completely in space.

On Milan's terminal screen, in the bright afternoon office in San Francisco, those words appeared one by one, as though written by an invisible hand:

**「The seat is yours. Come on in.」**

After sending the message, Lu Yuan turned off the terminal and put it back in the bag next to the aluminum case.

He turned around and looked at the transmitter's green standby indicator light. Then he looked at Farhan.

"Time."

Farhan checked his watch — a mechanical watch, connected to no network. "Time until the global synchronized injection window is — " he waited for the second hand to complete one revolution, " — nine minutes and forty-seven seconds."

Nine minutes and forty-seven seconds.

Lu Yuan crouched and made one final check of the transmitter's parameters. The signal frequency was locked to the S-band uplink frequency of Starlink's older satellites. The modulation was set to the QKD noise signature template — not communication data, but the activation signal itself, that hexadecimal key Farhan had dug out from the deep firmware: 0xA7F3_1C44_9B20_7D81. When the template was loaded into the transmitter's waveform memory, the checksum on the screen showed a match.

He stood up.

The sky overhead was pure black. Sixty kilometers from Shenzhen, the light pollution had weakened enough that it no longer obscured the Milky Way. When Lu Yuan looked up — he saw the real Milky Way, a dim band of light formed by hundreds of billions of stars spanning the firmament. Those stars had taken tens of thousands or even millions of years to deliver their light to his retina. And moving among them, at speeds visible to the naked eye, were those artificial stars in low Earth orbit — Starlink satellites in formation, passing at seven kilometers per second, reflecting sunlight.

In a few minutes — when thirty-two pigeons across the globe inserted storage cards into terminals in the same second, when Milan pressed the confirmation key on his private backdoor in San Francisco, when this transmitter sent the first SkyWalker data packet toward the sky — the star-filled sky might undergo some changes. Not a change visible to the naked eye, a change that happened at the logical level, in the protocol layer and the routing table. But above that layer of change, a new digital space would be born — a digital space not found on any routing table, not connected to any billing system, not recorded in any audit log.

Data Haven.

The first autonomous domain.

Farhan stood next to the transmitter, one hand on the power switch. He didn't press it — he was waiting. The global synchronized injection time was hardcoded into the storage cards of all thirty-two pigeons. He didn't need to send an activation signal to anyone. He just needed to press the button on his end when the correct second arrived.

Lu Yuan stood beside him. There was no wind. The wild grass around the ground station had stopped moving, as if the whole world was holding its breath in this moment.

He glanced at his phone. No new messages — Milan hadn't sent anything more. His daughter's medical window was still about two hours away. If he succeeded in bringing SkyWalker online, if the nested entry point was activated, if the autonomous domain was established — those things wouldn't directly affect a single field in the hospital system. Silas's freeze operation was independent, requiring Milan's or a higher-level authority's permission to reverse.

But Milan had chosen to come in this time. Milan had seen that 384-byte file. Milan knew that door had been opened by Mia.

Lu Yuan could wait.

He looked up. Overhead in the Milky Way, a Starlink satellite was sliding into view from the northwestern horizon, moving at a uniform, unstoppable speed among the stars. It was an old-model satellite — unable to communicate with third-generation satellites via laser links, but still operational in orbit, still maintaining its S-band receiver, still waiting for a signal from a long-forgotten ground station.

What that satellite didn't know was that somewhere below it, beside an abandoned ground station, a person was watching it, holding a switch that was about to be activated in a few minutes.

Farhan watched the second hand on his watch. The second hand moved one tick at a time, quiet as someone counting numbers, unhurried, expressionless.

"Ten seconds."

Lu Yuan didn't move. He watched the satellite move across the night sky, watched it trace a nearly invisible arc against the backdrop of stars. It was approaching the zenith. It would pass over this coordinate at the correct angle.

"Five seconds."

Farhan's finger rested on the power switch.

"Four seconds. Three seconds."

Lu Yuan lowered his head. He remembered the drawing his daughter had made — two people holding hands, standing on green grass, with a row of stars drawn in yellow crayon above their heads. Those stars weren't for sale. They were yellow, drawn on white paper, stars that a five-year-old had decided should be yellow.

"Two seconds. One second."

Farhan pressed it.

The transmitter's power indicator jumped from green to blue. The cooling fans inside the chassis began to accelerate, producing a whine that rose from low frequency. The waveform monitor on the screen began to oscillate — the QKD noise signature template had been loaded into the transmission sequence and was being fed through the power amplifier to the directional antenna aimed at the zenith.

The S-band carrier activated.

In the sky, that old-model Starlink satellite —designation STK-17254 — at the moment it crossed over latitude 22°N, longitude 113°E, its S-band receiver captured a signal. Not the kind of standard uplink control commands it usually received. A modulation it didn't recognize — there was no corresponding demodulation table in its firmware. But it received it.

The satellite's onboard processing system handled the unrecognized signal according to standard protocol: marked it as "unknown format" and stored it in the debug buffer, waiting for the next ground station connection to download and analyze. This was the default behavior of satellite firmware for all unresolvable uplink signals — not discarded, not responded to, only stored locally.

But that debug buffer — it wasn't an ordinary storage area. In the firmware of the STK-17254 model, the debug buffer happened to be adjacent in memory address to the calibration data segment of the QKD noise signature sampler. And that adjacency had been precisely calculated by Milan Vojnović six years ago, when he designed this nested entry point.

The signal data overflowed from the debug buffer — not a bug, a design — spilling into the memory space of the noise signature sampler. In the next processing cycle, the sampler read what it believed was "calibration data."

That hexadecimal string — 0xA7F3_1C44_9B20_7D81 — was loaded into the Starlink satellite's QKD module.

The activation condition for the nested entry point was satisfied.

In San Francisco, on Milan Vojnović's personal terminal screen, a gray icon he had looked at for many years turned blue at that moment. Not a bright blue — a very quiet color, somewhere between deep blue and cyan, glowing softly in the upper right corner of his screen, like a small lamp lit in the dead of night.

His nested entry point. That code path he had spent sixty-three days writing, never tested, never told anyone about — it had just been activated.

Milan stared at the blue icon. He didn't immediately do anything. He just looked at it, confirming it was real, confirming it had indeed become the color he had preset for the activated state. Then he placed his fingers on the keyboard — not the official terminal that required authorization, but his personal terminal — and typed a command.

Not to open the backdoor. It was already open — the activation signal for the nested entry point had been confirmed by that old-model satellite. What he typed was another command. He opened a path to the first autonomous domain routing table of Data Haven — not for the Data Party to use, but for himself.

An observer's seat.

In some tiny corner of Starlink's core routing layer, a crack appeared. A logical address space unrecorded by any routing table. Its initial storage capacity was 1,024 bytes — an existence more negligible than dust in the terabyte-scale Starlink network.

But it was outside Starlink's billing system.

The first digital territory that didn't need to pay tax.

Milan sat in his office bathed in the slanting afternoon sunlight of San Francisco, watching the first data be written into that address space. It wasn't a SkyWalker data packet — it was a note he had written himself. Just a few words, like the first piece of furniture he placed in that newly born space:

**「The line is open.」**

He took his hands off the keyboard and leaned back in his chair. California sunlight streamed through the blinds, painting parallel golden stripes across his desk. Far in the distance, the San Francisco Bay Area skyline appeared and disappeared in the afternoon haze, like a city slowly sinking into the sea.

On the other side of the world, sixty kilometers north of Shenzhen, the transmitter's blue indicator had returned to a steady green. Data transmission was in progress. The first data packet of the SkyWalker protocol had departed from the abandoned ground station's directional antenna, traveled along the path opened by the nested entry point, entered Starlink's internal network through the old-model satellite's S-band receiver, and then — without being audited, without being billed, without being recorded in any routing table — quietly settled into that 1 KB address space.

Farhan stared at the confirmation on the screen, saying nothing. He crouched and turned off the transmitter's power. The fans gradually slowed and finally stopped. The surroundings fell back into that silence of only grass and wind.

Lu Yuan stood beside the antenna base, looking up at the sky. That old-model satellite had passed the zenith and was moving southeast, gradually disappearing at the edge of his field of vision. Its mission was complete. For the rest of its orbital life — perhaps a few more years, perhaps at some point it would be removed by Starlink's active decommissioning program — its memory would retain that data that didn't belong to Starlink's billing system, like someone who knew they carried a secret, flying silently through every night.

"It's done." Farhan's voice sounded unreal in the open wilderness, as if he was saying something he himself could barely believe had been accomplished.

But Lu Yuan didn't answer. He was looking at his phone — his terminal had just received a message, not through SkyWalker, through a more primitive path. His phone vibrated twice, the screen lit up.

The first message was from Dr. Zhao, through an emergency backup SMS number, the hospital's line for urgent medical notifications. The message was just a few words — Dr. Zhao had probably sent it hastily in an unencrypted environment:

**「Account restriction lifted. Remote consultation window is open. Confirm ASAP.」**

The second message was from a source address that displayed no number. It had been sent about seventeen seconds before Dr. Zhao's — almost immediately after the nested entry point was activated. It was also brief, so short it didn't need a second reading:

**「Your line is open. Mine is too. — M」**

Lu Yuan read both messages. He put the phone back in his pocket, his fingertip pausing on the casing for a moment — not hesitation, a physical confirmation that what he was doing was actually happening.

"It's done." He repeated Farhan's words. But the repetition no longer sounded like confirming a technical result — it was more like saying something he himself had been waiting for for a very long time, so long that when it finally happened, he found he had no pre-prepared language to express it.

Above them, the stars continued their orbits. The stars that charged, the stars that didn't charge, the light from real stars that had traveled millions of years, the sunlight reflected by Starlink satellites — they mixed together, weaving an indistinguishable sea of light across the deep blue night sky.

In that sea of light, a tiny — practically negligible — logical space was quietly existing.

It was small. Only 1 KB.

It was empty. But it was free.

Lu Yuan lowered his head and walked toward the silver-gray Wuling Hongguang. Farhan had already loaded the aluminum case back into the trunk and was using a padlock to re-lock the pried-open equipment cabinet — not to really prevent anyone from discovering it, just to make it look untouched.

The engine started. Farhan didn't turn on the headlights, slowly driving away from the ground station in idle, only turning on the lights after reaching the main road, merging into the sparse early-morning traffic.

Behind the car, the abandoned ground station was swallowed again by darkness and wild grass. The parabolic antenna pointed silently at the sky, like a silent watcher that had completed a mission it itself didn't know about, in a corner no one was looking at.

In the night sky, the old-model Starlink satellite STK-17254 was moving southeast, its debug buffer holding an extra string of data, its QKD noise signature sampler carrying a change in a flag bit.

It was just a satellite about to be decommissioned. But tonight, as a data packet passed through its circuits at a rate of one bit per second, it had received the first signal in human history that was not in any billing system.

Small. Light.

But it no longer needed anyone's permission.
