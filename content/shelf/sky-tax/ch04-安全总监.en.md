# Chapter 4 · The Security Director

Silas Chen was reading a transfer request when the alert came.

An amber marker box popped up in the bottom-right corner of his screen—not the highest red alert, not the ignorable gray, but a subtle intermediate color meaning "worth noting" but "not an immediate threat." The title bar read:

**"Behavioral Pattern Anomaly · Classification: A3 · Manual Review Recommended"**

Silas set down the transfer request. He tapped the touchpad, and the marker expanded to full-screen, revealing the underlying data analysis panel. Three side-by-side information windows displayed: user identity profile, connection behavior heatmap, and anomaly score radar chart.

The user identity profile summary was simple:

**User ID: PLT-EU-4421-ANV**
**Tier: Platinum**
**Registered Location: Antwerp, Belgium**
**Activation Date: March 2054 (three years and two months ago)**
**Device Type: OrbitNet Home Hub Gen3**
**Account Status: Normal · No Arrears · No Violations**

On the surface, nothing was wrong. A well-established European Platinum user, the account clean as a new mirror. But Silas knew that the cleanest accounts were often the most suspicious. A real user always left traces—occasional overuse, occasional password resets, occasional customer service complaints. Clean to this degree meant either a bot was operating it, or it was a shadow account with something to hide.

He switched to the behavior heatmap.

The moment he did, his pupils contracted slightly.

The heatmap showed the user's activity distribution over the past seventy-two hours. During European hours—UTC+1 daytime—the terminal's usage was very regular: three to four hours of medium-to-high-intensity use between 9 AM and 1 PM daily, primarily enterprise video conferencing and cloud collaboration platform traffic. But starting four days ago, during UTC+8 late-night hours—European deep night—sustained high-intensity usage had suddenly appeared, with a data traffic pattern completely different from the European daytime: HD video tutorials, online education platforms, medical consultations, large-scale file downloads.

Silas hovered his cursor over the downloaded file type statistics and expanded the detailed list. He scrolled through it line by line, and his expression gradually darkened.

The downloaded documents included: *Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Refractory Childhood Epilepsy (2056 Revised Edition)*, *OrbitNet AI Medical Image Analysis API Technical Manual*, *Chinese Medical Association Expert Consensus on Pediatric Neurology*, *A Survey of Deep Learning-Based Detection of Abnormal EEG Discharges*—over fifty Chinese medical literature PDFs, each ranging from several megabytes to dozens of megabytes.

A Belgian Platinum user, at three in the morning, searching and downloading the latest Chinese pediatric neurology research papers in fluent Chinese.

Silas leaned back in his chair, took off his glasses, wiped them with a cloth, put them back on, and looked again. The data hadn't changed. Neither had the conclusion.

He pressed the intercom: "Liu Yang, pull the authentication event sequence for PLT-EU-4421-ANV over the past four days. I need it down to every handshake protocol. Also retrieve the physical-layer signal fingerprint match rates."

"Copy that." The voice on the other end was crisp.

Silas stood up and walked to the monitoring room's floor-to-ceiling window. Outside was the skyline of Shenzhen's Nanshan Science Park, dense skyscrapers like integrated circuit boards stuck into the earth, every building blazing with round-the-clock white light. Starlink's Asia-Pacific Security Center was housed in an unremarkable gray building on the southern side of the Science Park—from the outside, indistinguishable from the surrounding office towers. But four floors underground was the real heart: a three-thousand-square-meter cybersecurity monitoring center, analyzing the connection data streams of 230 million Starlink users across Asia-Pacific in real time.

Silas Chen was the director of that heart.

Forty-two years old. Ethnic Chinese Singaporean. Former technical officer in the Singapore Air Force's Signal Intelligence unit. Recruited by Starlink at triple his previous salary to oversee Asia-Pacific network security. He'd been in this role for six years and seen every kind of intrusion attempt—from Burmese hackers trying to forge Silver-tier tokens, to Indonesian communication traffickers using cracked terminals to resell quotas. Most of the time, he snuffed out problems before they became crises.

But this time was different.

This time, the technique was too clean. Signal fingerprint match: 99.94%. Authentication handshake: zero failures. Timing jitter: entirely within normal range. If the behavioral pattern analysis algorithm hadn't caught those Chinese medical literature downloads, this cloned signal might never have triggered any alarm at all.

He walked back to his workstation. Liu Yang had already sent the authentication event sequence, along with a set of physical-layer raw logs. Silas opened the logs. Lines of hexadecimal data and timestamps scrolled across the screen. He didn't need tools to parse this—the skill he'd developed in the Air Force was finding the signal in all the noise.

He found a faint wrinkle.

Forty-seven seconds after the connection was established, the signal underwent an extremely minute phase jump. The jump's amplitude: only 0.3 degrees. Duration: under two milliseconds. Virtually undetectable to the naked eye on a spectrogram, but the raw data left a trace—a characteristic of a buffer queue overflow in the digital signal processing path, an instantaneous jitter from a relay node handling high-throughput data.

This signal had passed through at least one relay. Not a Starlink-authorized ground relay station—those had signal characteristics Silas could recognize blindfolded. This was a non-standard device, deployed at an unknown location, using illegal means to accomplish signal relay and cloning.

Silas didn't act hastily. He closed all analysis windows, leaned back, crossed his arms, closed his eyes, and thought for a while.

His first instinct was to report it to Belgrade. By Starlink's internal procedures, security events involving the physical-layer authentication system had to be copied to the Chief Protocol Architect's office—that is, Milan Vojnović. But the thought lingered in his mind only a few seconds before being replaced by another.

Milan wouldn't give him the credit he deserved.

Silas knew his position in Starlink well. He was the Asia-Pacific Security Director, but to the people at headquarters, he was just a hired hand—a Singaporean, former Asian military, not part of the inner circle. Every time he submitted a security report, the emails he cc'd to Milan vanished into a void. The occasional replies were just a few words of acknowledgment—never asking for details, never expressing interest.

But this time was different. This time he was holding a real thread.

If he could trace that thread to its source, if he could catch the cloner himself, if he could close the case without alerting anyone—then in the eyes of headquarters, he would no longer be just a hired hand.

He opened his eyes and created a new tracking task, codename: "Net Strike."

In the task description, he wrote:

**Objective: Locate physical coordinates of non-standard signal relay device. Scope: Nanshan District, Shenzhen, OCT to Science Park area. Strategy: Passive cross-location, no active probing. Constraint: Must not trigger anomaly status on target device. Priority: Maximum. Report scope: Restricted to this unit only.**

He did not check the box to cc Milan.

---

Lu Yuan was completely unaware of any of this.

He was experiencing the most peaceful few days in nearly five years.

His daughter's condition seemed to have entered a brief stable period. After that high-quality remote consultation over the Platinum network, the young female doctor with glasses had prescribed a new medication regimen—including two more precisely targeted antiepileptic drugs and a personalized monitoring plan based on OrbitNet AI, which automatically adjusted medication dosage every day based on his daughter's EEG data and heart rate variability. This plan was impossible to run on a Copper-tier network—it required continuous data upload and real-time AI inference feedback, each inference consuming tens of thousands of tokens. On a Copper account, a single inference meant a three-day wait.

But on the Platinum network, it was instantaneous.

Every morning, the first thing Lu Yuan did was check his daughter's monitoring report on the tablet. EEG data was automatically collected, uploaded, and analyzed through the night. At 6 AM sharp, a diagnostic recommendation was generated. The first day's report showed a 12% reduction in abnormal discharge frequency. The second day: 21%. The third day's report had an additional line at the bottom, in green font from the AI system:

**"Patient is responding well to the current regimen. Continue current plan and reassess in three days."**

Lu Yuan stared at that green line and felt a stone that had been pressing on his chest for years lift by one centimeter. Not much. Just one centimeter. But it was enough for him to breathe.

He decided to take his daughter to the park.

It was Saturday. Shenzhen in June was an oven, but the OCT Ecological Park was thick with trees, a breeze blowing across the lake, the temperature at least five degrees cooler than the city center. Lu Yuan put a sun hat on his daughter, packed water and snacks, and took three stops on the bus to the park entrance.

He bought two tickets—fifteen credits each, his daughter was free. The ticket seller glanced at him, then at the girl, and said with a smile, "Your kid's got good color." Lu Yuan smiled back. "She's been doing better lately."

Inside the park, his daughter held his hand and walked along the tree-lined path, her steps lighter than last month. She was wearing a white floral dress Lu Yuan had bought for thirty credits on a secondhand platform, washed clean and looking lovely on her. She stopped under a large banyan tree, looking up at the sparrows hopping between the branches, utterly absorbed.

"Daddy, what are they saying?"

"Maybe they're saying the weather is nice today."

"Do they know we're watching them?"

"They probably do. Birds have sharp eyes."

His daughter thought for a moment, then asked a question that made Lu Yuan's heart sink: "Daddy, what about those stars—do they know we're watching them?"

She meant the Starlink satellites. The park had no tall buildings blocking the sky. At dusk, you could occasionally see moving points of light gliding across the sky at a uniform speed—not airplanes, those had blinking lights. These had constant brightness, constant speed, and there were many of them.

"They..." Lu Yuan paused, "of course they don't know."

He looked up as he said it. Right overhead, a Starlink satellite was slowly crossing the sky. In the afterglow of sunset, it reflected the sun's light, like a solitary traveler walking across a deep blue curtain.

He didn't know that satellite was indeed "watching" him.

He didn't know that, in the celestial net woven by one hundred twenty thousand satellites above, a tracking program codenamed "Net Strike" had pinpointed his approximate coordinates three hours ago—error margin: two hundred meters. By the time he walked into the park with his daughter, the location marker on the monitoring system had tightened to within fifty meters.

Silas Chen was watching his trajectory from the monitoring room.

The map on the screen read: **Target moving within Shenzhen OCT Ecological Park. Suspected to be accompanied by a child. Activity pattern—leisurely walk. Assessment: Non-immediate threat. Continue observation.**

Silas watched the moving dot travel slowly along the park's forest paths, occasionally stopping—probably because the child had stopped to look at something. A cup of iced American coffee from the convenience store downstairs sat beside his hand, condensation beading on the cup, leaving a wet ring on the desk.

He did not order an arrest.

Arrest would be easy. Shenzhen was one of Starlink's most heavily monitored cities. The Asia-Pacific Security Center could directly mobilize local cyber enforcement forces—the target would be surrounded within twenty minutes. But Silas didn't want to spook the game. What he wanted to confirm wasn't just "who is cloning the signal," but "how did they do it?" Was there a larger network behind it? An organized chain? Or just a lone individual?

If it was the latter, he wanted to know if this person was worth recruiting.

No—what he wanted to know was whether this technique could become *his* leverage.

Silas took a sip of iced coffee and said to Liu Yang, who was sitting beside him, "Zoom in on his activity trajectory. All location data from the last seven days. Pull as much as you can."

"Chief, that requires retrieving the complete connection logs of all his downstream devices. It's a lot of data."

"Then pull it. I'll sign off on the authorization."

Liu Yang said nothing and began typing. On the screen, data fragments from different time slices were assembled like puzzle pieces, gradually outlining Lu Yuan's recent activity: his repair shop address, his rental location, the market he frequented, the pharmacy where he bought medicine, the hospital he'd taken his daughter to—

The hospital data made Silas pause.

He zoomed in on that coordinate and cross-referenced the date. The target had visited Shenzhen Municipal Children's Hospital four days ago, neurology department, visit duration: two hours and seventeen minutes. He searched the hospital's internal system for the terminal's network activity records at that time—he couldn't access the full medical data; Starlink's user data privacy protocols limited his depth of query—but he saw an index: the account had attempted to request an OrbitNet AI advanced diagnostic, which had been rejected by the system due to insufficient quota.

Silas pieced these pieces together in his mind. Repair shop. Children's hospital. Medical literature downloads. Rejected AI diagnostic request.

A profile gradually took shape. This didn't look like a professional communications thief. This looked more like... a father with no way out.

Silas leaned back in his chair, stared at the ceiling, and was silent for a while. He didn't know where the thought came from, but once it appeared, it wouldn't leave. He was a security director. His job was to freeze this illegal connection, locate and arrest the intruder, and block all associated terminals. That was the standard procedure.

But his index finger hovered over the "Execute Block" button and didn't press it.

He needed a more complete answer. Who this person was, how he'd done it—and most critically, whether this technical method could be replicated by others.

He didn't intend to hand that answer to Milan. He intended to keep it for himself.

He closed the operations interface, stood up, and walked to the window. Outside, the Science Park was lighting up for the evening, the lights of countless office buildings merging into a sea of illumination. The cloner was hidden somewhere in that sea, like a fish that had dived into the deep ocean.

Silas looked out the window, his Adam's apple moving slightly.

"I want him alive," he murmured, as if saying it to the air, or to himself. "I want to know how he did it."

Behind him, Liu Yang heard the words but didn't respond. He kept staring at the positioning dot on his screen—the target was still in the park, walking by the lake with a small child, completely unaware that he was already locked in the sights of countless digital eyes.

---

In the park, dusk was falling.

Lu Yuan crouched down to retie his daughter's loosened shoelace. The girl stood before him, her small hands on his shoulders, and suddenly said:

"Daddy, I want to be a doctor when I grow up."

Lu Yuan looked up at her.

"Why?"

"Because doctors can help people get better," she said very seriously, not at all like a five-year-old. "The lady doctor today smiled at me. Her smile was so pretty. I want to make other kids smile too."

Lu Yuan's hands stopped on the shoelace. He didn't look up, afraid she would see his eyes.

"Okay." His voice was hoarse. "Then you need to eat well, sleep well, and grow up strong."

"Mm!"

The girl nodded firmly, then turned around and took her father's hand, continuing to walk along the lakeside. The lake reflected the city's skyline and the rising moonlight, ripples fine as countless fragments of hammered silver.

Lu Yuan held her hand and walked through the twilight. Overhead, the next Starlink satellite was already emerging on the eastern horizon, a cold point of light, at a uniform speed, without emotion, drawing closer.

He didn't know that point of light was being read.

He didn't know that the program codenamed "Net Strike" had already locked onto every step he took.

But at this moment, he didn't care.

The only thing he cared about right now was the warmth coming from his daughter's palm—small, warm, seeping into his bloodstream along the lines of his hand, grain by grain, like some precious, slowly infused strength.

The stars above were watching him. But his hand was holding his daughter's.

That was enough.
